Clarity Analytics: what it is, how it works, and where it goes next

A plain-language tour of the platform that turns your calls, support chats, and orders into dashboards and predictions, plus a concrete plan for running it on live data.

What this thing actually does

Three jobs, one platform.

Every day, customers call us, chat with support, and place (or cancel) orders. All of that used to sit in raw tables nobody could read. Clarity Analytics reads it for you and answers three questions:

  • What are customers telling us? Every support chat and call transcript gets read by AI and tagged: is the customer happy or angry, what triggered it, was it resolved, did we breach SLA.
  • Where is the pain concentrated? Dashboards slice everything by time, zone, merchant, and vertical (Restaurants, Grocery, Market, Health & Wellness) so you can see where problems cluster, not just that they exist.
  • Which orders are about to cancel? A prediction engine scores live orders for cancellation risk, so someone can step in before the customer gives up.

The tech stack, in human terms

Four building blocks. You don't need to touch any of them, but it helps to know what each one is when engineers mention them.

Next.js + React + Tailwind

The dashboards (frontend)

The website you actually open. Built with the same tooling as most modern web apps. Handles the English/Arabic toggle, the global time filter (WTD/MTD/QTD/YTD), charts, and the notification bell. Think of it as the storefront.

FastAPI (Python)

The backend (API)

The kitchen behind the storefront. When a dashboard needs numbers, it asks this service. It fetches data, does the math, caches results so pages load instantly, and keeps popular queries pre-warmed every 4 minutes.

Google BigQuery

The data warehouse

Where all the raw material lives today: chat history, call records, order data, and every AI classification we've produced. It's built for huge historical analysis, less so for split-second live lookups (more on that in section 6).

Google Gemini

The AI layer

Gemini (the same family of AI as ChatGPT) does the reading and the judging. It reads chats and transcripts like a human would: sentiment, topic, resolution. It also looks at an order's details and history to judge cancellation risk and explain its reasoning in plain words.

Honest limitations of the current setup

This is a strong analytics prototype. Here's what it is not yet.

  • It's snapshot-based, not live. Chats are classified by a daily pipeline; dashboards show "as of last run." A spike at 2pm shows up tomorrow, not at 2:05pm.
  • BigQuery is built for history, not the hot path. It's brilliant at "show me six months of trends" and slow/expensive at "check this one order right now." The 4-minute cache warmer exists precisely to paper over this.
  • Predictions score a queue, not the order stream. The risk queue re-scores a batch of orders; a truly live system would score each order the moment it's placed.
  • Login is a placeholder. A shared dev password stands in until Google OAuth credentials arrive. Fine for a demo, not for production.
  • A timezone quirk needs care: chat timestamps are stored in UTC while order times are Qatar local (UTC+3). The code compensates, but a live system should normalize this at the source.

The upgrade: going live with Postgres / AWS

The core idea: keep BigQuery for history and heavy analysis, add a fast operational database (PostgreSQL on AWS RDS) for the live, right-now data. Two databases, two jobs.

Concrete ideas, roughly in order of payoff

Score every order at placement time Biggest win

Instead of re-scoring a queue, the order system pings the scoring service the moment an order is placed. High-risk orders appear on the Cancellations page within seconds, while there's still time for an agent or an automated nudge (a proactive "your order is being prepared" message) to save it.

Postgres (RDS) as the hot store Foundation

Keep the most recent ~90 days in PostgreSQL on AWS RDS. Dashboards read live counts from Postgres instantly; BigQuery keeps its job as the deep-history archive. This also kills most of the caching gymnastics the backend does today.

Real-time chat sentiment, not daily CX team

Classify chats as they close (or even mid-conversation for long ones) instead of once a day. An angry-sentiment spike about a specific merchant becomes an alert the CX lead sees within minutes: early warning for outages, bad batches, courier shortages.

Push alerts to where agents already work Call center

The notification bell is a start; the next step is pushing high-risk-order and SLA-breach alerts into the tools agents actually stare at all day (Slack/Teams/the CS console), with a one-click "I'm on it" that feeds back into the data.

Close the loop: did the intervention work? Accuracy

When an agent acts on a risk alert, record what they did and whether the order survived. That outcome data is gold: it shows which risk calls Gemini gets right, sharpens the patterns we feed it, and finally answers "is this tool actually saving orders?" with a number.

Refresh the risk patterns monthly Accuracy

Customer behavior shifts (Ramadan, new zones, new merchants). Once a month, re-pull the cancellation patterns from BigQuery history and update what Gemini is told to look for, so its risk calls don't go stale.

Production hygiene Before launch

Turn on real Google OAuth login, normalize all timestamps to one timezone at ingestion, set up AWS-side monitoring/backups for RDS, and keep the existing Gemini cost guardrails (minimal thinking mode, batch API for bulk work); the AI bill is a real budget line.

Why not just move everything to Postgres? Because the two databases are good at opposite things. Postgres answers "what's happening right now" in milliseconds; BigQuery answers "show me every pattern in two years of data" without breaking a sweat. Using each for its job is cheaper and faster than forcing either to do both.

TL;DR

Clarity Analytics already turns raw chats, calls, and orders into dashboards the CX and call-center teams can act on, with Gemini doing the reading and flagging at-risk orders. The architecture is solid but snapshot-based. The path to production is clear: stream events into a Postgres hot store on AWS, score orders the moment they're placed, alert agents in real time, and keep BigQuery as the long-term memory the risk patterns are drawn from. Nothing gets rebuilt; the live layer slots in front of what already works.
